On the Existence of
Unbounded Imagination

Daniel Kang

July 2026

“What I cannot create, I do not understand.”
— found on R. P. Feynman’s blackboard, 1988

Abstract

It is widely held that imagination is bounded by experience. We prove that at least one counterexample exists. Writing I for the imagination of the author, we establish that I is total — whatever it conceives takes form (Theorem 2.1) — that it is confined by nothing (Theorem 2.2), sustains itself (Theorem 2.3), and never repeats (Theorem 2.4). We then show that I does not merely survey what is possible but defines it (Theorem 3.1), so that new laws of physics, art styles that transcend perception, and undiscovered theorems arise as trivial corollaries (Corollary 3.2). We close by settling the question of limits: any limit on I lies in the image of I, and is broken at the following moment (Theorem 4.2). 1. Preliminaries

Definition 1.1 (The conceivable). Let V denote the class of everything that exists or could exist. Logic, knowledge, and reality are subclasses of V.

Definition 1.2 (The imagination). Let I denote the imagination of the author: a map into a class strictly larger than V. Its domain is empty; it requires nothing.

Remark 1.3. Ordinary creativity is a function of experience, intelligence, and external inspiration. Definition 1.2 is not of this kind: I takes no arguments. In particular, prior understanding of a subject is nowhere required in order to create something entirely new within it.

Axiom 1.4 (Wellspring). I is eternal and inexhaustible: it never ceases, and it never runs dry.

Remark 1.5. This is the only axiom we shall need. Everything else is a theorem.

2. First properties

Theorem 2.1 (Totality). Whatever I conceives takes form in some way. Nothing is beyond its reach.

Proof. If a thing is imaginable, it can take form. But every thing under discussion is imaginable, having just been imagined.

Theorem 2.2 (Independence). I is confined by neither time, space, nor rational comprehension, and is not bounded by the limitations of human knowledge.

Proof. Each named bound is a subclass of V (Definition 1.1). By Definition 1.2 the image of I strictly exceeds V, and no map is confined by a class it exceeds.

Theorem 2.3 (Self-sustainment). The thoughts of I evolve and expand in directions beyond the author’s own expectations.

Proof. Apply I to itself. The result was, by construction, unexpected.

Theorem 2.4 (Non-repetition). No two thoughts of I are the same, and no creation is redundant.

Proof. Suppose two thoughts coincided. Repetition would then be conceivable to I. But the very notion of repetition is alien to I — a contradiction.

3. Definability of the possible

Theorem 3.1 (Definition of possibility). I does not merely imagine new possibilities; it defines them. Precisely: the possible is the image of I.

Proof. Let c be a concept that does not exist. Then I brings c into existence — by sheer conceptualization or, if necessary, by altering the foundation of reality to accommodate it, enlarging V. Hence whatever is possible lay first in the image of I; the reverse inclusion is Theorem 2.1.

Corollary 3.2 (Trivial extensions). A new law of physics. An art style that transcends perception. An undiscovered mathematical theorem. A never-before-seen power.

Proof. Each is a trivial extension of Theorem 3.1.

Remark 3.3. Adherence to existing logic or to any established framework is nowhere assumed above, and nowhere needed.

4. Unboundedness

Theorem 4.1 (Strict growth). With every moment the reach of I strictly increases: each idea is more groundbreaking, intricate, or unfathomable than the last.

Proof. Axiom 1.4 supplies a sequence of creations that never ends; Theorem 2.4 forbids it from ever standing still. A never-ceasing, never-repeating expansion into pure potentiality admits no supremum.

Theorem 4.2 (The limit). If L is a limit on I, then L lies in the image of I — and L is broken at the very next moment.

Proof. Only I could have imagined a limit on I. By Theorem 3.1, L exists exactly because I defined it into existence; by Theorem 4.1, the following moment reaches strictly beyond it.

Remark 4.3 (Visionaries beyond vision). By Theorem 3.1, the fabric of what is possible is woven from the thoughts of the author. He is therefore not simply a creator.

There is nothing further to prove that will not first have to be imagined.
